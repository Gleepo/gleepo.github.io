How to use this

Pinyin layer: hover any Chinese text (or tap it, on iPad) to see its reading. The button at the right of the menu bar cycles between hover, always-shown, and off. Readings are dictionary tones — tone sandhi is not applied except in a few fixed phrases — so 你好 shows as nǐ hǎo, and Part D of the Phase 1 manual is where the spoken form lives.

This is Phase 0 — the revision layer that sits underneath the Phase 1 reading manual. It covers HSK 1–2 ground you have already met and lost, so the work here is reactivation rather than learning. That is a genuinely different and faster process; expect things to come back rather than go in.

Keep it open while you work. Looking things up here is the intended behaviour. Nothing in this manual asks you to retrieve anything cold.

Order matters and is not the usual one. Sounds first, then the phrases that keep a conversation alive, then grammar, then vocabulary. Most courses put vocabulary first. That order is wrong for your goal: you can hold a real conversation with a small vocabulary and good repair strategies, and you cannot hold one with a large vocabulary and none.

If you get pulled away: note the part letter and the last checkpoint you passed. Every part is self-contained, so re-entry costs nothing.

What this phase is actually for

The goal is conversations with your parents. That reshapes everything. It means listening matters more than reading, that home vocabulary beats classroom vocabulary, and that fluency is less about knowing more and more about recovering fast when you have lost the thread.

It also means you have something most learners do not: a native-speed source who is on your side, will repeat things, and is not going anywhere. That is the best language resource that exists, and it is the reason Part B is worth more than the whole rest of this document.

Part A — The sounds pinyin lies about

Why start here

Pinyin uses Roman letters for sounds those letters do not make in English. If you learned the spelling and quietly kept an English pronunciation underneath it, that mismatch is invisible on the page and audible the moment you speak. Six of these account for most of it.

Say every example aloud. This section cannot be done silently — it is the only motor-skill part of Phase 0.

1. j / q / x are not English j, ch, sh

They are made further forward, tongue flat and low, tip near the bottom teeth. q in 请 qǐng is closer to a hissed "ch" said with a smile than to "chuh." x in 谢谢 xièxie is a hissed "sh" made at the same forward position.

Watch out: after j, q, x, a written u is always ü. 去 is not "qoo" — it is qù with the ü of French tu. Same for 学 xué, 觉 jué.

2. zh / ch / sh / r are retroflex — tongue curled back

Contrast them against z / c / s, which are flat and forward. 四 sì (four) versus 是 shì (to be). 早 zǎo versus 找 zhǎo. The pair 四 / 十 (sì / shí) is the classic one and worth drilling on its own.

r in 热 rè is not an English r and not a French r — it is the retroflex position with voice, closer to the "s" in "measure" said with a curled tongue.

3. -n and -ng are different words, not accents

很 hěn (very) and 行 xíng (okay). 山 shān (mountain) and 上 shàng (on, up). English lets these blur; Mandarin does not. For -n the tongue tip touches behind the teeth; for -ng it does not touch at all and the sound resonates at the back.

4. Written vowels that hide a sound

ui is really uei: 对 duì sounds like "dway," not "dwee." iu is really iou: 六 liù sounds like "lyoh." un is really uen: 春 chūn has a small e in the middle.

5. -ian sounds like "yen"

天 tiān is "tyen," not "tyahn." 钱 qián, 年 nián, 边 biān all follow. But -iang keeps the "ah": 想 xiǎng, 两 liǎng. This one pair of finals catches almost everybody.

6. Bare e is not "eh"

Alone, e is a back unrounded vowel, roughly the "u" in "duh." 了 le, 的 de, 呢 ne, 喝 hē, 热 rè. It only becomes "eh" in ei (给 gěi) and ie (谢 xiè).

Tones themselves — including the three sandhi rules that written pinyin does not show — live in Part D of the Phase 1 manual. If your tones feel shaky, do that section before this one; it is the other half of this part.

Checkpoint A

  1. Say aloud, slowly: 四 sì — 十 shí — 是 shì — 吃 chī — 七 qī. Five sounds, three tongue positions. If two of them came out identical, that pair is your homework.
  2. Say 去 qù and 天 tiān aloud. Check yourself against the rules: did 去 have a ü, and did 天 rhyme with "yen"?
  3. Say 很好 hěn hǎo and 行 xíng back to back. Feel where your tongue tip is on each ending. If it is in the same place both times, one of them is wrong.

These are self-checks and self-checks are unreliable for sound. Once this week, record thirty seconds of these and play it back — or, better, say them to your parents and let them tell you.

Part B — Staying in the conversation

Why this is the most important part of the manual

Conversations with fluent speakers do not fail because you lack vocabulary. They fail at the moment you stop understanding, freeze, and switch to English — after which the Chinese conversation is over and it is very hard to restart.

Repair phrases break that chain. They convert "I am lost" from a conversation-ending event into an ordinary turn that keeps you inside the language. Learn these to automaticity before anything else in this document. Fluent-sounding repair also buys you enormous patience from the person you are talking to, because it signals you are still in the game.

Stated plainly: knowing these ten phrases cold is worth more to your goal than the next three hundred words of vocabulary.

When you did not catch it

你说慢一点儿，好吗？ Nǐ shuō màn yìdiǎnr, hǎo ma? Could you speak a bit slower? The single highest-value sentence here. Use it early and often — most people speed back up within a minute, so you will use it repeatedly, and that is normal rather than rude.
请再说一遍。 Qǐng zài shuō yí biàn. Please say that again. 遍 biàn is the measure word for a repetition of something from start to finish. Note 一 becomes yí here, before the fourth tone.
我听不懂。 Wǒ tīng bu dǒng. I hear it but I don't understand it. Different from 我不懂 (I don't understand at all). 听不懂 says specifically that the sound reached you and the meaning didn't — which tells your parents to rephrase rather than just repeat louder.
等一下。 Děng yíxià. Hang on a second. Buys you processing time without ending your turn. Underrated — most of what feels like not understanding is just not understanding fast enough.

When you caught it but need the meaning

这个词是什么意思？ Zhège cí shì shénme yìsi? What does this word mean? 词 cí is a word; 字 zì is a character. Use 词 when you heard something, 字 when you saw something written.
⋯⋯用中文怎么说？ … yòng Zhōngwén zěnme shuō? How do you say … in Chinese? Your main engine for growing vocabulary in conversation instead of in a book. Say the English word in the blank; nobody minds.
你的意思是⋯⋯？ Nǐ de yìsi shì …? Do you mean …? Say back what you think you heard. This is how you check comprehension without stopping the conversation, and it gives your parents a chance to correct you gently.

When you need to keep going anyway

我明白了。 Wǒ míngbai le. Got it. / Now I understand. The 了 matters: it marks the change from not-understanding to understanding. 我明白 without 了 sounds oddly flat.
我的中文不太好，但是我在学。 Wǒ de Zhōngwén bú tài hǎo, dànshì wǒ zài xué. My Chinese isn't great, but I'm learning. Worth having ready as a whole unit. 不太好 is softer than 不好. 在学 uses 在 for an ongoing action — see Part C.
我们用中文说，好不好？ Wǒmen yòng Zhōngwén shuō, hǎo bu hǎo? Let's talk in Chinese, okay? The one that makes the rest possible. If a conversation has drifted into English out of habit, this pulls it back — and asking explicitly is far more effective than trying to hold the line silently.

Checkpoint B

  1. Cover the pinyin. Say all four "when you did not catch it" phrases aloud from the characters. If you cannot, uncover and say them again reading — reading them is a pass at this stage.
  2. Say aloud the difference between 我听不懂 and 我不懂. (The first says the sound arrived but the meaning didn't; it asks for rephrasing rather than volume.)
  3. Pick the three you would most likely need in your next real conversation, and say each one five times. Not read — said, out loud, at conversational speed. These need to be reflexes, not lookups.

Item 3 is the one worth actually doing. A repair phrase you have to look up is not a repair phrase.

Part C — HSK 1–2 grammar, as frames

Why frames rather than rules

You have met all of this before. It is coming back, not going in — so the job is to give each pattern one clean slot-shaped form you can drop words into, rather than re-teach the reasoning behind it.

Read each frame, then say one sentence of your own out loud using it, about something in the room. Out loud.

主语 + 动词 + 宾语 Subject + Verb + Object

The spine. Time and place go before the verb, not after — this is the word-order habit English speakers break most often.

我今天在家吃饭。I'm eating at home today. (Not: I eat at home today — the time and place both precede the verb.)
A 是 B A shì B

Links two nouns. Never use 是 with an adjective. "I am tired" is not 我是累.

他是我爸爸。He is my dad.
主语 + 很 + 形容词 Subject + hěn + adjective

Adjectives act as their own verb. 很 is usually just a connector here and does not really mean "very" — 我很累 is "I'm tired," not "I'm very tired."

我很累。今天很冷。I'm tired. It's cold today.
有 / 没有 yǒu / méiyǒu

To have; to exist. The one verb negated with 没 rather than 不, always.

我没有时间。家里有人吗？I don't have time. Is anyone home?
在 + 地方 / 在 + 动词 zài + place / zài + verb

Two jobs. Before a place: located at. Before a verb: happening right now.

我在家。我在做饭。I'm at home. I'm cooking.
动词 + 了 verb + le

Marks a completed action. Not a past tense — it marks completion, which is why it turns up in sentences about the future too. Negate with 没 and drop the 了.

我吃了。我没吃。I've eaten. I haven't eaten.
⋯⋯吗？ / ⋯⋯呢？ … ma? / … ne?

吗 turns any statement into a yes/no question with no word-order change. 呢 bounces a question back: "and you?"

你累吗？我很好，你呢？Are you tired? I'm good, and you?
动词 + 不 + 动词 V + bu + V

The other yes/no question. 你去不去？ is the same question as 你去吗？ and sounds more natural in speech.

你要不要喝茶？Do you want tea?
想 / 要 / 会 / 能 / 可以 xiǎng / yào / huì / néng / kěyǐ

想 = would like to. 要 = want to, will (stronger, sometimes blunt). 会 = know how to, or will (future). 能 = physically able to. 可以 = permitted to.

我想学中文。我会说一点儿。I'd like to learn Chinese. I can speak a little.
有点儿 vs 一点儿 yǒudiǎnr vs yìdiǎnr

This is the HSK 2 trap. 有点儿 goes before an adjective and carries a note of complaint: 有点儿贵 (a bit too expensive). 一点儿 goes after and is neutral comparison: 便宜一点儿 (a bit cheaper). Swapping them is the single most common HSK 2 error.

今天有点儿冷。请说慢一点儿。It's a bit cold today (and I don't love it). Please speak a bit slower.
A 比 B + 形容词 A bǐ B + adjective

Comparison. No 很 in this pattern — 我比你高, never 我比你很高.

今天比昨天冷。Today is colder than yesterday.
太⋯⋯了 tài … le

Too / so. The 了 is not optional in speech.

太好了！这个菜太好吃了。That's great! This dish is so good.
⋯⋯的时候 … de shíhou

When / while. The whole clause comes first, then 的时候, then the main sentence.

我小的时候住在这儿。When I was small I lived here.
数 + 量词 + 名词 number + measure word + noun

You cannot count a noun directly. The ones you need at home: 个 (general), 位 (people, polite), 杯 (cups), 碗 (bowls), 双 (pairs), 件 (clothes), 张 (flat things), 只 (animals), 本 (books), 条 (long things).

两杯茶，一碗饭。Two cups of tea, one bowl of rice. (Note 两, not 二, before a measure word.)

Checkpoint C

  1. Say aloud: "I'm a bit tired today." Check it against the 有点儿 frame — the 有点儿 must come before 累. (我今天有点儿累。)
  2. Say aloud: "I haven't eaten yet." Check it against the 了 frame — did you correctly drop the 了 when you negated? (我还没吃。)
  3. Say aloud one sentence using 的时候 about your childhood. Check the order: clause, then 的时候, then the rest.

Items 1 and 2 are the two errors most likely to survive from your earlier study. Say them, then actually check them against the frame rather than assuming.

Part D — The words that actually come up at home

Why this list is not the HSK 1–2 list

The HSK 1–2 wordlists are built for a student's life: classrooms, teachers, classmates, plane tickets, hotels. That vocabulary is fine and you will meet it again. It is not what gets spoken between parents and their adult child.

What follows is HSK 1–2 vocabulary filtered and reweighted for the actual setting: family, food, health, home, weather, time, feeling. Roughly 150 words. It is deliberately smaller than a full HSK 2 list, because a small set you can produce beats a large set you can only recognise.

The word doing the most work here

Checkpoint D

  1. Cover the pinyin column of the Family table and read the characters aloud. Uncover and check. Recognising them counts as a pass.
  2. Say aloud three 怎么样 questions you could genuinely ask your parents this week.
  3. Say 买 and 卖 aloud, one after the other. If they sound the same, that is a tone problem rather than a vocabulary problem — third tone dips and rises, fourth tone falls hard.

Do not try to hold this whole table. It is a reference. The words you actually use will stick from use, and the rest are here for when you need them.

Part E — Ten things you can say this week

Why whole sentences rather than building blocks

Building a sentence from grammar under time pressure is slow, and the pause is where conversations die. Whole memorised openers cost you nothing at the moment of speaking and buy the time your parents need to shift into Chinese with you.

These are not exercises. They are things to actually say. Pick two, use them this week, and let the rest wait.

你吃了吗？ Nǐ chī le ma? Have you eaten? Functions as a greeting more than a question about food. One of the most natural things you can open with, and it will be immediately recognised as such.
你最近怎么样？ Nǐ zuìjìn zěnmeyàng? How have you been lately? Open question, invites a real answer. Follow it with 呢？ to bounce back when they ask you.
你身体好吗？ Nǐ shēntǐ hǎo ma? How's your health? Not intrusive in Chinese the way it can read in English — asking after health is ordinary care between family.
你今天做了什么？ Nǐ jīntiān zuò le shénme? What did you do today? Uses the 了 completion marker from Part C. Note the 了 sits on the verb, not at the end.
我今天很忙，但是还好。 Wǒ jīntiān hěn máng, dànshì hái hǎo. I've been busy today, but I'm alright. A ready-made answer to the question you will get back. Having your own answer prepared is half of what makes a conversation feel possible.
这个菜真好吃，是你做的吗？ Zhège cài zhēn hǎochī, shì nǐ zuò de ma? This is really good — did you make it? Uses the 是⋯⋯的 focus pattern (Phase 1, Part C). Food is the safest and richest conversational territory available to you; use it.
外面很冷，你多穿一点儿。 Wàimiàn hěn lěng, nǐ duō chuān yìdiǎnr. It's cold out — put on another layer. Care expressed as instruction is completely normal register between family in Chinese. This will land warmly, not bossily.
我很想你们。 Wǒ hěn xiǎng nǐmen. I miss you both. 想 does the work of "miss" with no extra verb. Short, direct, and difficult to say by accident — which is rather the point of learning it deliberately.
这个用中文怎么说？ Zhège yòng Zhōngwén zěnme shuō? How do you say this in Chinese? Repeated from Part B on purpose. Pointing at an object and asking this turns any room into a lesson, and it puts your parents in the role of teacher, which most parents enjoy.
我想跟你们多说中文。 Wǒ xiǎng gēn nǐmen duō shuō Zhōngwén. I want to speak more Chinese with you. Say this one first, before any of the others. It sets the terms for everything that follows, and it is the sentence most likely to change how the next year of conversations goes.

Checkpoint E

  1. Pick two from the ten. Say each aloud five times until it runs without stopping.
  2. Say aloud what your answer would be if your parents replied to 你最近怎么样？ with a question back. If you don't have one, build it now from the Part C frames — a prepared answer is not cheating.
  3. Decide when this week you will use them, and with which parent. Say the day out loud. An undated intention is not a plan.

Item 3 is the checkpoint that matters. Everything else in this manual is preparation for it.

Checkpoint F

  1. Answer twenty. The quiz takes about four minutes.
  2. Write down — on paper, not mentally — every word you missed. That list is your actual revision set, and it will be much shorter than you fear.
  3. Stop at twenty even if you want to continue. Ending on a finished chunk beats a long blurred session.

Where this sits

Phase 0 is this document: reactivating HSK 1–2 and getting you into live conversation as fast as possible. Phase 1 is the reading manual — radicals, sound families, HSK 3 frames, tone pairs. Phase 2 is handwriting, which you have chosen to defer.

Stated plainly, because it is the whole strategy: Part B and Part E of this document are the ones that move you toward actual conversations with your parents. Everything else supports them. If you only ever do two sections of this manual, do those two.

Nothing here needs finishing before you start talking. The conversations are the method, not the reward for completing the method.
