How to use this

Pinyin layer: hover any Chinese text (or tap it, on iPad) to see its reading. The button at the right of the menu bar cycles between hover, always-shown, and off. Readings are dictionary tones — tone sandhi is not applied except in a few fixed phrases — so 你好 shows as nǐ hǎo, and Part D of the Phase 1 manual is where the spoken form lives.

Keep this open while you study. It is a reference card, not a memory test. Looking something up here is the intended behaviour, not a failure. Free recall will arrive on its own through repetition; nothing in this manual depends on you retrieving anything cold.

Work one part at a time. Each part ends with a checkpoint — a short, concrete, do-it-out-loud check. Run the checkpoint even if you feel finished. That is the point of it.

If you get pulled away: note the part letter and the last checkpoint you passed. Stop there. Re-entry costs nothing because every part is self-contained.

Nothing here is saved anywhere. The quiz in Part E resets when you reload, by design — it is a warm-up, not a record.

The idea this manual is built on

Why bother with a system at all

Most learners at your level treat each new character as a separate item to memorise. That is roughly 3,000 unrelated things, and it is the hardest possible version of the task.

But Chinese characters are not arbitrary pictures. Around 80% of them are compounds: one part tells you roughly what the character means, and another part tells you roughly how it sounds. Once you can see the seam, a character stops being a shape and becomes a small sentence about itself.

The plain implication: learning about 30 meaning-parts and a few dozen sound-parts gives you partial access to thousands of characters you have never seen. That is a far better return than learning 200 more characters one at a time.

Take 清, 请, 情, 晴, 睛. Five different characters. Every one of them contains 青 (qīng), and every one of them is pronounced somewhere in the qing / jing neighbourhood. The left-hand part is what changes the meaning: water, speech, heart, sun, eye.

So 清 is qīng, water + qing = clear. 请 is qǐng, speech + qing = to request. 情 is qíng, heart + qing = feeling. You did not memorise five things. You memorised one thing and five modifiers.

Part A gives you the meaning-parts. Part B gives you the sound-parts. Part C gives you the sentence frames to put them in. Part D is the speaking work, which is a separate skill and lives at the back on purpose.

Part A — The 29 radicals that do the most work

Why these twenty-nine

There are 214 traditional radicals. Most are rare. These twenty-nine are the ones that appear again and again in HSK 1–4 characters, and each one is a reliable clue to a whole semantic zone. When you meet an unknown character, finding the radical is the first move — it narrows the meaning before you know anything else.

Reading the table: the first column is the radical as it appears inside a character, which is often a squashed version of a standalone character. Where the two differ, the standalone form is named in the third column. This is exactly the kind of small visual difference that is easy to skim past, so it is spelled out rather than left to be noticed.

The one that will trip you

月 is genuinely two radicals wearing the same shape. In 明 (bright) and 期 (period) it is the moon. In 脸 (face), 胖 (fat), 腿 (leg), 肚 (belly) it is 肉, flesh. If a character with 月 in it seems to be about the body, it is — you have not misread it.

Checkpoint A

  1. Cover the fourth column. Look at 药 and say out loud which zone of meaning its radical points to, then uncover and check. (Grass. It is a herbal thing. It means medicine.)
  2. Do the same for 睡, 钱, and 病 — say the zone aloud before checking the table.
  3. Say aloud, in one sentence, what 氵 and 讠 have in common structurally. If your answer mentions the position they sit in, you have it.

If any of those three failed, re-read the relevant table row before moving on. A failed check is the system working.

Part B — Sound families

Why this is the highest-leverage part of the manual

Radicals tell you meaning. Sound-parts (声旁, shēngpáng) tell you pronunciation, and almost nobody teaches them explicitly — which is why most learners at HSK 2–3 are still memorising pronunciations one at a time.

The rule is loose but real: if a character contains a component that is itself a character, that component is often carrying the sound. Tone frequently changes; the initial and final usually survive. Treat it as a strong hint, not a law.

Where the rule breaks, stated plainly

生 → 星 xīng is a sound shift you would not predict from scratch (sheng to xing), and 青 → 睛 jīng is another. That is normal. The sound-part narrows the possibilities; it does not always name the answer. Use it to reduce a blank to a short list, and let context or a dictionary close the last step. A hint that works four times in five is still worth having.

Checkpoint B

  1. Look at 泡. Say out loud, before checking: which part carries the sound, which part carries the meaning, and what the meaning zone is. (包 carries sound; 氵 carries meaning; it is about liquid.)
  2. Here is a character you were not taught above: 饭. Name its radical and its meaning zone out loud. Then check Part A. (Food radical 饣 — it means cooked rice, a meal.)
  3. Say aloud one sentence explaining why 请 and 情 sound almost identical but mean unrelated things. If your sentence uses the words "same sound-part, different radical," you have it.

Item 2 is the real test — it asks you to use the system on something new rather than recall something taught. If it worked, Part B has done its job.

Part C — Twelve sentence frames

Why frames rather than grammar rules

At HSK 3 the bottleneck stops being vocabulary and becomes structure — you know the words but cannot assemble them past a simple sentence. Frames fix this faster than rules do, because a frame is a slot you drop known words into rather than a principle you have to apply under pressure.

Read each frame, then say one sentence of your own out loud using it. Out loud, not in your head — the whole point is to make the structure automatic before you need it.

虽然⋯⋯但是⋯⋯ suīrán … dànshì …

Although X, (but) Y. Both halves stay in Chinese, unlike English where you drop one.

虽然很贵，但是我要买。Although it's expensive, I want to buy it.
因为⋯⋯所以⋯⋯ yīnwèi … suǒyǐ …

Because X, therefore Y. Same doubling as above. Cause first, result second — always.

因为下雨，所以我没去。Because it rained, I didn't go.
一边⋯⋯一边⋯⋯ yìbiān … yìbiān …

Doing two things simultaneously. Both slots take verbs.

他一边吃饭一边看电视。He eats while watching TV.
越来越⋯⋯ yuè lái yuè …

More and more X, over time. Takes an adjective or a verb of feeling.

天气越来越冷了。The weather is getting colder and colder.
越⋯⋯越⋯⋯ yuè … yuè …

The more X, the more Y. Different from the frame above — this one links two variables, not one variable to time.

我越听越喜欢。The more I listen, the more I like it.
除了⋯⋯以外，⋯⋯ chúle … yǐwài, …

Besides X / except X. Which of the two it means depends on what follows: 都 signals "except," 还/也 signals "besides."

除了他以外，我们都去。Everyone except him is going.
除了中文以外，他还会法语。Besides Chinese, he also speaks French.
S + 把 + O + V + 结果 S bǎ O V + result

Moves the object in front of the verb to say what was done to it. The verb can never stand bare — it needs a result, direction, or 了 attached. This is the single most-tested HSK 3 structure.

我把作业写完了。I finished writing the homework.
S + 被 + Agent + V + 结果 S bèi Agent V + result

Passive. Same rule about the verb needing a completion. Used far less than the English passive, and often carries a whiff of something unwelcome happening.

我的手机被他拿走了。My phone was taken away by him.
V + 得 + 形容词 V + de + adjective

Describes how an action is performed. If the verb has an object, the verb repeats: 他说中文说得很好.

她跑得很快。She runs very fast.
是⋯⋯的 shì … de

Focuses on when, where, how a completed past action happened — not on whether it happened. The thing being emphasised sits between 是 and 的.

我是坐飞机来的。I came by plane (that's how I came).
快要⋯⋯了 / 就要⋯⋯了 kuàiyào … le / jiùyào … le

About to happen. 就要 can take a time word in front of it; 快要 cannot.

火车快要开了。The train is about to leave.
一⋯⋯就⋯⋯ yī … jiù …

As soon as X, then Y. Tight sequence, no gap implied.

我一到家就给你打电话。I'll call you as soon as I get home.

Checkpoint C

  1. Say out loud one 把 sentence about something on your desk right now. Check it against the rule: does your verb have something attached after it? If the verb is bare, the sentence is wrong — add 了 or a result.
  2. Say out loud the difference between 越来越 and 越⋯⋯越 in one sentence. (One tracks change over time; the other links two variables.)
  3. Turn this into a 是⋯⋯的 sentence out loud: "I bought it yesterday." (我是昨天买的。)

Item 1 is the one people rush. Say the sentence, then actually look at your verb.

Part D — Tone pairs, for speaking

Why pairs and not the four tones

You can almost certainly produce each of the four tones correctly in isolation. That is not where intelligibility breaks down. It breaks down in combination, because your mouth has to reset pitch between syllables at speed, and some transitions are much harder than others.

Drilling the twenty pairs is the standard fix. Each cell below gives you a real two-syllable word to use as the anchor for that transition. Say the word, not the tone numbers.

Announced transition: this is a different kind of work from Parts A–C. Those were visual and analytical. This is motor practice. Do not try to do both in one sitting.

The three sound changes nobody writes down

These are called 变调 (biàndiào), tone sandhi. They are not optional or regional — they are how the language is actually spoken, and the written pinyin usually does not show them.

3 + 3 → 2 + 3

Two third tones in a row: the first becomes a second tone. Written 你好 nǐ hǎo, spoken ní hǎo. Same for 很好 (hén hǎo), 我想 (wó xiǎng), 水果 (shuí guǒ).

不 bù → bú before tone 4

不是 is bú shì, not bù shì. 不去 is bú qù. Before tones 1, 2, 3 it stays bù: 不吃 bù chī, 不来 bù lái, 不好 bù hǎo.

一 yī shifts by context

Alone or counting: yī. Before a tone-4 syllable: yí — 一样 yíyàng, 一个 yíge. Before tones 1, 2, 3: yì — 一天 yìtiān, 一年 yìnián, 一起 yìqǐ.

Checkpoint D

  1. Say all five tone-3-first words aloud in a row: 老师, 起床, 你好, 考试, 姐姐. Listen for whether 你好 came out as ní hǎo. If it came out as two full third tones, say it again — that dip-dip is the single most audible learner tell.
  2. Say 不是, 不去, 不好, 不吃 aloud. Two of those four are bú and two are bù. If you had to stop and think, that is fine — say the set again until it runs without stopping.
  3. Record yourself saying any five words from the grids on your phone, then play it back. Playback, not memory: your judgement of your own tones while producing them is unreliable, which is the entire reason for this step.

Item 3 is the one that gets skipped. It is also the only one in this manual that gives you outside evidence rather than self-report. Do it.

Part E — Recognition warm-up

Why recognition and not recall

This quiz shows you a character and asks you to pick its meaning from four options. That is deliberate. Recognising the right answer among distractors is a legitimate measure of knowing a character, and it is the form of knowing that reading actually requires. Producing it from a blank page is a separate, much harder skill, and Phase 2 is where that belongs.

Getting one wrong tells you which table to reread. That is all it means.

After you answer, a line of feedback appears under the buttons and the "Next character" button becomes the way forward — it sits at the bottom-left of the box, beside the running score.

Checkpoint E

  1. Answer fifteen. Not five — fifteen. The point is repetition volume, and the quiz takes under three minutes.
  2. For every one you got wrong, name aloud which part of this manual it came from (radical table, or a sound family) and scroll back to that part.
  3. Stop after fifteen even if you want to keep going. Ending on a completed chunk is worth more than a long session that blurs.

What Phase 2 will be

You chose recognition first, handwriting later. That was the right order for how you learn, and the reason is worth stating explicitly rather than leaving implied: this manual is built almost entirely out of recognition tasks, which is your strongest memory channel. Handwriting is the opposite — free recall, stroke by stroke, with no cue on the page.

When you add it, Phase 2 will be built differently: stroke-order rules as a small set of principles rather than per-character diagrams, a grid practice sheet, and a much smaller character set worked much more slowly. Roughly 40 characters, not 200. Handwriting done properly is slow, and pretending otherwise is how people abandon it.

Nothing in Phase 2 depends on finishing Phase 1. They are separate tracks and can overlap.
