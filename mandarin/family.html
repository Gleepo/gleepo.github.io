Addendum — Your Family's Mandarin

The short version

Pinyin layer: hover any Chinese text (or tap it, on iPad) to see its reading. The button at the right of the menu bar cycles between hover, always-shown, and off. Readings are dictionary tones — tone sandhi is not applied except in a few fixed phrases — so 你好 shows as nǐ hǎo, and Part D of the Phase 1 manual is where the spoken form lives.

Standard Mandarin is the right target and nothing about your family changes that. Your mother was educated in Beijing; your step-father's Mandarin is Northeastern, which is one of the closest regional varieties to the standard. Both are within easy reach of what the Phase 0 and Phase 1 manuals teach.

The one thing that does change: your step-father is your best practice partner, and you should start with him. Northeastern Mandarin is grammatically almost identical to the standard and phonetically very close. Your mother's Mandarin carries a southern substrate that makes some sounds less clear as a model — not worse, but less useful as a template while you are rebuilding your ear.

And one thing to stop worrying about: your aunt and uncle. If they sound like locals, they are speaking something Mandarin will not unlock. Details below, but the practical point is that failing to understand them is not a measure of your progress.

Your mother's side — Meicheng, Jiande, Zhejiang

Two languages, not one accent

梅城, 建德, 浙江 — on the Xin'an River 新安江

Meicheng is a town in Jiande, in Hangzhou prefecture. The local speech, Jiande dialect, is classified as Wu Chinese — specifically within the Wuzhou (婺州) group of Southern Wu, centred on Jinhua, and sometimes grouped instead into a proposed Jinqu subfamily. The classification is genuinely disputed among linguists; the branch is not.

The thing to understand about that: Wuzhou Wu is described as only poorly intelligible even with Taihu Wu, the northern branch that includes Shanghainese, Suzhou and Hangzhou speech. It is a separate language from Mandarin by any ordinary measure — it preserves voiced initial consonants that Mandarin lost, and runs seven or eight tones against Mandarin's four.

Stated plainly: learning Mandarin will give you no access at all to your mother's childhood dialect. That is not a failure of your plan, it is a fact about the languages. If you ever want that dialect too, it is a separate and much harder project — Wuzhou Wu has essentially no learning materials, no standard written form, and no courses.

What a Wu background typically does to someone's Mandarin

Your mother studied at Beijing Jiaotong University, so she has years of immersion in northern Putonghua sitting on top of a Wu-speaking childhood. "All over the place" is exactly what that produces, and it is extremely common — she will move between registers depending on who she is talking to.

The features below are what a Wu substrate typically leaves behind. Treat this as a list of things to listen for, not a description of her:

FeatureWhat you'd hear
Retroflexes softenWu has no zh / ch / sh / r series, so they often merge toward z / c / s. 是 shì drifts toward sì; 知道 zhīdào toward zīdào.
-n and -ng blurThe distinction that Part A of Phase 0 insists on is weaker in southern speech. 很 / 行 may sound closer together than the manual implies.
Little or no erhuaSouthern speakers largely drop the 儿 suffix. 一点儿 becomes 一点, 哪儿 becomes 哪里.
n / l — probably notAn n/l merger is common in Hunan, Hubei and Sichuan speech but is not a typical Wu feature; Wu varieties generally keep them distinct. Listed here so you don't go looking for it.
我们 over 咱们咱们 is a northern habit. Southern speakers use 我们 for both inclusive and exclusive "we."

The implication for your own pronunciation, stated outright

If your early Mandarin came from your mother, some of these features are probably in your mouth too — most likely the soft retroflexes and the weak -n/-ng contrast. Both are on the Phase 0 Part A trap list.

That is not an error you made. It is an accent you inherited, and it is a real and normal way that hundreds of millions of people speak Mandarin. Whether you want to move toward the northern standard or keep it is a preference, not a correctness question. What matters is that you know which features are yours, so you can hear them.

Your step-father's side — Shenyang, Liaoning, with Beijing above it

Northeastern Mandarin (东北话)

沈阳, 辽宁 — father from Beijing, mother from Liaoning

This is good news for you. Northeastern Mandarin is a Mandarin variety, not a separate language — grammar essentially identical to the standard, vocabulary mostly shared, phonology close. It is generally reckoned one of the easiest regional accents for a learner of Standard Mandarin to follow.

A Beijing father and a Liaoning mother in a military family also means a household that would have used Putonghua as a matter of course. Military families were posted across the country and standard speech was the norm rather than the exception.

What to listen for

FeatureWhat you'd hear
Heavy erhuaThe 儿 suffix on far more words than the textbook uses, especially from the Beijing side. 一会儿, 好玩儿, 这儿, 味儿.
Swallowed syllablesA Beijing habit. 不知道 compresses toward something like "bùr-dào"; 我们 toward "wǒm". This is the single hardest thing about Beijing speech for learners, and it is a compression, not a different word.
Flat / retroflex swapsNortheastern speakers sometimes have z/c/s where the standard has zh/ch/sh, or the reverse — but per word rather than across the board.
Tone contour differencesThe four tones are the same tones, but the pitch shapes sit slightly differently. Your ear adjusts to this quickly and it rarely blocks comprehension.
咱 / 咱们 everywhereInclusive "we." 咱们一起去 means "let's go, you and me." Worth adding to your active vocabulary — it sounds warm and it is a northern marker.

Why this table earns its place

None of these are in any HSK list, and several are among the most frequent words in casual northeastern speech. 咋 alone will appear more often in an hour of family conversation than most of the HSK 2 wordlist. Knowing ten of them turns a wall of unfamiliar speech into standard Mandarin with ten known substitutions in it.

What to call people

Why this needs its own section

Chinese kinship terms encode which side of the family a person is on and their age relative to your parent. There is no general word for "aunt" or "uncle" — the term tells you the relationship. Getting these right is noticed, and getting them wrong is the most visible kind of learner error inside a family.

Parents

TermPinyinNotes
妈 / 妈妈mā / māmaThe safe default everywhere. 妈 alone is the normal spoken address.
娘niángNorthern, and now reads as older or rural. Your step-father's region uses it. It would sound affectionate and slightly old-fashioned rather than wrong — but it is not what you would call your own mother if she is southern-raised.
爸 / 爸爸bà / bàbaThe default for a father you address as a father.
叔叔shūshuAlso used for a step-father or a mother's partner where "dad" isn't the relationship. Neither choice is more correct — this is about your relationship, not about Chinese.

The formal word for step-father, 继父 jìfù, is a term you would write on a form, not one you would ever say to his face. Whatever you call him in English is the better guide to which of the two above to use.

Grandparents — the side matters

Your family straddles that north/south line, so both sets may be in use around you for different people. Neither is more correct; follow whichever your family already uses.

Your aunt and uncle in Meicheng are on your mother's side, so 舅舅 and 舅妈, or 姨妈 and 姨父, depending on which of them is the blood relative. If you have been calling them something else your whole life, that is what they are — an inherited family term outranks a table.

Checkpoint

  1. Say aloud what you would call your mother's brother. (舅舅 jiùjiu.) Then his wife. (舅妈 jiùmā.)
  2. Say aloud the two different words for your maternal grandmother, and which side of the country each comes from. (外婆 wàipó, southern; 姥姥 lǎolao, northern.)
  3. Decide, and say out loud, what you are going to call your step-father in Chinese. There is no correct answer and it is worth having settled before the conversation rather than during it.

Item 3 is the one that will otherwise ambush you mid-sentence. Two seconds of deciding now.

Who to practise with, in what order

1. Your step-father. Closest to the standard you are learning, and Northeastern speech has a reputation for volubility that works in your favour — you want a partner who keeps talking. Use the Part E openers on him first.

2. Your mother. Her Mandarin is fully accessible to you; the substrate features above just make her a slightly less clean phonetic model. As a conversation partner she is likely the more patient one, and she is the one who can tell you which of your own sounds came from her.

3. Your aunt and uncle — later, and with different expectations. If they speak local Mandarin heavily coloured by Jiande dialect, you will follow much less of it, and if they switch into the dialect proper you will follow none. Neither of those is a progress signal. Use your repair phrases and let them slow down for you.

One concrete thing worth doing early, because it costs one conversation and saves months: ask your mother which sounds of yours came from her. Something like 我的发音有没有浙江口音？ Wǒ de fāyīn yǒu méiyǒu Zhèjiāng kǒuyīn? — "does my pronunciation have a Zhejiang accent?" She will know instantly, and it is a question she will probably enjoy.

What I don't know

Flagged rather than guessed

How much dialect your mother actually retains. Someone who left for university in Beijing may speak the home dialect fluently with her siblings and barely at all otherwise. That affects nothing about your Mandarin plan, but it affects what is available to you if you ever want more.

Whether your aunt and uncle speak Jiande dialect or accented Mandarin — "sound like locals" could mean either. The first is inaccessible to you; the second becomes accessible with practice. Worth finding out, because the two lead to very different expectations.

Which specific features are in your own speech. Nothing in this document can tell you that. Your mother can, in about thirty seconds.

The Jiande dialect classification itself. Wuzhou Wu versus a Jinqu grouping is unsettled in the literature. It does not change anything practical — it is Southern Wu either way — but I would rather flag the disagreement than present one label as settled.
